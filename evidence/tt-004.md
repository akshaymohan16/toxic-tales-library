The approximately 8,000 fed eggs are derived from eight birds × approximately 500 eggs × two species trials. The birds were force-fed. The paper reports fertilisation success of 100% for common carp and 30% for Prussian carp; do not label all fed eggs viable or fertilised. Nine living recovered embryos died of fungal infection during incubation. Controls also had substantial fungal loss (50/50 common carp; 36/50 Prussian carp). These are protocol-specific counts, not an estimate of natural colonisation success.

Most recovered eggs passed within one hour. One common carp egg passed at four to six hours. The two Prussian carp hatched 49 hours after ingestion; the common carp hatched 68 hours after ingestion. No bird flight or actual inter-pond transport was measured. Distances inferred in the discussion are omitted from this Reel.

## Story decision and scope

The story starts with the surprising survival result, then establishes why it matters: dry land lies between ponds, while a duck can cross it. The experiment supplies evidence for one step in that possible route. Rarity and incubation are visible and audible. If the graphic shows 12 living embryos followed by three hatches, it must also show that nine died of fungal infection during incubation; this is not a natural survival-rate funnel. The ending returns to the pond-to-pond question and explicitly remains conditional.

Do not claim the experiment explains the origin of fish in any particular isolated lake, demonstrates successful wild establishment, or establishes how frequently this happens. Do not invent a protective egg coating or digestive mechanism. The narration does not say fish hatch inside ducks. All diagrams are original schematic explanations; reference wildlife footage is not labelled as documentation of the experiment.

## Caption draft

A duck's meal could be a fish's ride to a new pond. 🦆🐟

Dry land separates ponds, but ducks can cross it. Could fish eggs survive the journey inside a bird?

In a controlled study, eight mallards were force-fed roughly 8,000 common and Prussian carp eggs across two trials. Researchers recovered 18 intact eggs. Twelve held living embryos, and three hatched after incubation in water.

The result shows that some developing carp eggs can survive digestion. The experiment did not show ducks establishing fish populations in wild ponds. Fungal infection also killed many recovered and control embryos during incubation, so these counts are not a clean estimate of hatching success in nature.

Paper: Lovas-Kiss et al. (2020), PNAS 117(27), 15397–15399. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2004805117

## Research entry

Some fish eggs survived a journey through a mallard's digestive tract. In a captive experiment, researchers retrieved a small number of intact eggs from droppings; three later hatched after incubation in water. This reveals a possible way for eggs to cross the dry land between ponds, while leaving successful establishment in wild ponds untested.

**Demonstrated:** Survival of developing eggs of common and Prussian carp through captive mallards, followed by hatching after incubation.

**Not demonstrated:** A duck transporting these eggs on an actual flight between ponds; How often egg ingestion, deposition, hatching or establishment occurs in nature; The origin of fish in any particular isolated pond; A biochemical or anatomical explanation for egg survival.

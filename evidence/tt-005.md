## Numbers and scope that must not be mixed

The repeated-freeze trial lasted 19 days; estimated cumulative freezing was 15 days. Frogs were not held at −8°C throughout that period. The final −8°C hold was 5 hours. Normal behaviour was observed at the first examination around 30 hours after thawing began. Five of five monitored frogs remained healthy for 7 weeks.

The direct −8°C and −16°C groups were used for biochemical sampling. **The focal paper does not report a survival test for its −16°C group.** Earlier studies discussed there must not be combined with the focal five-of-five result.

Glucose and urea are contributors to freeze tolerance, not the entire adaptation. The focal study does not experimentally isolate their sufficiency for whole-animal survival. Species, population, season, cooling rate and preparation constrain the interpretation.

## Story and narration

The opening names a wood frog and asks how living cells survive ice inside the body. Seasonal preparation and on-demand sugar explain a protective strategy; the small survival test supplies concrete evidence, then the ending returns to winter survival. This is not a claim of resurrection, a human freezing technology or a freezing experiment for viewers to reproduce.

This wood frog can survive ice forming inside its body. How do its cells survive?

In Interior Alaska, winter survival starts before the first freeze.

The frogs build up urea. Then, as freezing begins, their livers turn stored glycogen into glucose: sugar.

Together, these substances limit ice formation and water loss from cells. They help the frog tolerate freezing, without stopping it completely.

In one experiment, researchers repeatedly froze and thawed winter-prepared Alaskan wood frogs, finally reaching minus eight degrees Celsius.

All five frogs checked for survival recovered and stayed healthy for seven weeks.

These were winter-conditioned Alaskan frogs; not every frog can do this.

Their winter strategy: protect living cells while part of the body freezes.
